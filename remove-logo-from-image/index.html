Logo types

Four Kinds of Logo, Four Kinds of Job

How well the mark comes off depends on what is behind it, not on how big it is.

Corner watermark badge

A small mark parked in one corner — stock libraries, supplier photos, AI tools. Usually the easiest case: the area under it is sky, wall or plain background nine times out of ten.

Brand mark across the product

A logo printed on the item itself — a bottle label, a cap, a tote. The model has to rebuild the material underneath, so expect a second pass on glass, metal or fine weave.

Text-only logo

Wordmarks, taglines, "photo by" credits. These behave like any other text, which is what the tool was built for — clean edges and a plausible fill behind the letters.

Repeated or tiled marks

Preview watermarks repeated across the whole frame are the hardest. Auto detection catches most of them; the leftovers in dense areas are quicker to brush one by one than to chase with a full-image pass.

Product shots, comps and catalog clean-up

The everyday use is unglamorous: a supplier sends product photos with their own logo stamped in the corner, and you need those images in your store, your deck or a client comp without a second brand sitting in the frame.

Two things make this work better than a clone stamp. First, the fill is generated from the surrounding pixels rather than sampled from one spot, so gradients and soft shadows survive. Second, you can process a handful of near-identical shots and get consistent results instead of a patch that looks different in every photo.

Comps are the other half: designers pull reference images into a layout and need them clean before a client review. Same upload, same one click.

Only for logos you own or are allowed to remove

This tool clears brand marks from images that are yours: your own photography, your own products, supplier photos whose licence lets you edit, or work you were commissioned to retouch. That is the whole intended use.

Removing someone else's logo or watermark so the image can be reused, resold or passed off as unowned is copyright infringement in most jurisdictions, and it breaks our terms of use. PixPurge does not provide a guide for that, and we do not help with takedown dodging.

If you are not sure whether you have the right, the honest answer is usually to ask the rights holder — or buy the licence. A clean file is not worth the claim.
